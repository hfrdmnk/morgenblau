The Quiet Software

by Ada Lovelace

Software, at its best, is calm. It does not shout, it does not blink, it does not draw attention to itself. The most useful tools in our lives are the ones we forget are tools at all — the kettle, the lamp, the bookshelf.

For decades, the dominant aesthetic of software has been the opposite of calm. Notifications stack, badges throb, animations demand the eye. Every product is a fight for attention, and attention is finite. We have built an entire industry on the premise that the loudest interface wins.

But there is another tradition, quieter and older, that runs through the work of people like Mark Weiser at PARC, who wrote in 1991 of "calm technology" — software that recedes to the background of consciousness, surfacing only when needed and otherwise leaving the user alone.

Calm technology is not a feature you bolt on at the end. It is an orientation, a set of choices made at every level of the stack. It is the decision not to send a notification. It is the decision not to add a badge. It is the decision to render a daily digest instead of an infinite feed.

The economic incentives, of course, run the other way. Loud software is easier to monetise — engagement is measurable, calm is not. The user who closes the app and goes for a walk does not generate a metric. The product manager whose interface generates fewer pings does not get promoted.

And yet there are signs that the tide is turning. The most thoughtful designers I know talk now about quietness, about reducing the surface area of their products. There is a small but growing readership for newsletters about software that respects the reader. There is an emerging market — perhaps a niche, perhaps the next mainstream — for tools that do less.

This is the world Morgenblau is trying to inhabit. Not the loud world, not the infinite-feed world, but the world where you open an app once a day, read what is there, and close it. The world where the work is in choosing what to show, not in maximising how long you stay.

I do not know if calm wins. I know that I want it to. And I know that every product built in this tradition is a small bet that the future of software is quieter than its present.

The kettle does not blink. The lamp does not buzz. Maybe, one day, neither will the phone.
